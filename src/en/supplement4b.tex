\section{The implication tested}

The claim of the previous section --- that the price of externalization is a transfer of
$\phi_{\rm barg}$ --- can be tested directly. Following the discipline of
Part~\ref{part:method}, the implication was fixed before the survey.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Registered implication}: a platform's fee rate increases monotonically in the number
of terms the platform absorbs.
\end{quote}

The alternative is stated explicitly: fee rates are set by market power rather than by the
functions absorbed. That is, the very decomposition of Chapter~\ref{sec:surplus} into
$\phi_{\rm prod}$ and $\phi_{\rm barg}$ is at issue.

Two comparisons were specified in advance for identification.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item Same function, different scale. If the rates are close, the function account holds; if
they differ greatly, the market-power account does.
\item Differences in rate within one operator. Whether there is a difference the function
account cannot explain.
\end{enumerate}

This survey falls under none of the obstacles of Section~\ref{sec:obstacle-taxonomy}: the
price lists are public, and neither per-firm data nor confidentiality is involved.

Only the last term differs in character. Customer acquisition is not the operation of a
contract but the winning of customers, and belongs not to $C_{\mathrm{admin}}$ but to the
$T_{\rm acq}$ of~\eqref{eq:E-required-2}.
\textbf{The externalization price of $C_{\mathrm{admin}}$ is about 11 points; $T_{\rm acq}$
alone is 20.}
\end{remark}

The ladder is broadly monotone. Stripe absorbs payment only, at a flat 3.6\% for domestic
cards, debit, wallets and convenience-store payment. The merchant-of-record providers Paddle
and Lemon Squeezy charge 5\%${}+{}$50¢ and collect and remit sales tax in more than 200
jurisdictions. The increment from Stripe to a merchant of record can be read as the price of
$T_{\rm inst}$. For an individual selling into Europe, the alternative is registering for VAT
in each country or paying an accountant monthly, so a price of 1.4--2.4 points is consistent
with a return to function.

\section{A natural experiment within one platform}

This is the most important observation. Gumroad's 30\% Discover fee applies only when the
customer arrived through Gumroad's marketplace; purchases through one's own link or an
external route are charged the standard 10\%. Discover can also be switched off.

\textbf{The same platform, the same market power, the same other functions. The only
difference is whether $T_{\rm acq}$ was supplied.}

\begin{equation}
\text{price of } T_{\rm acq} = 20.0\ \text{points}
\label{eq:tacq-price}
\end{equation}

The market-power account cannot explain this difference: one and the same firm charges the
same customer different rates according only to whether it performed the acquisition.
Equation~\eqref{eq:tacq-price} is the strongest evidence for the implication.

Moreover the standalone price of $T_{\rm acq}$, 20 points, exceeds the sum of all the other
items (about 11 points, from 3.6\% to 14.5\%).
\textbf{Customer acquisition is the most expensive item to externalize.} The judgment in
family 6-4 that $T_{\rm acq}$ is the dominant component is supported in direction.

\section{A counterexample: price discrimination by size}

At the same time, a case the function account cannot explain was obtained.

The App Store Small Business Program cuts the fee from 30\% to 15\% for developers earning
under one million dollars a year. Paid apps, in-app purchases and subscriptions are all
covered.

\textbf{The functions supplied are exactly the same and only the rate differs, by a factor of
two.} A 15-point difference cannot be a return to function; it is price discrimination by
size.

The circumstances of its introduction are also suggestive. The programme was announced in
2020, when antitrust actions by the US Department of Justice and the European Commission
coincided with the dispute with Epic Games.
\textbf{Pressure from the institutional layer moved the rate.}

In the decomposition of~\eqref{eq:surplus}, the level of platform fees can therefore be
written
\begin{equation}
\phi_{\text{fee}} = \underbrace{\phi_{\rm prod}}_{20.0\ \text{pt}} + \underbrace{\phi_{\rm barg}}_{15.0\ \text{pt}}
\label{eq:fee-decomp}
\end{equation}
the first from the within-platform comparison on Gumroad and the second from the App Store's
two size tiers.
\textbf{This is the only case in this text where two of the three components were separated
numerically for a single transaction.}

\begin{remark}[\eqref{eq:fee-decomp} is incomplete]
\label{rem:fee-incomplete}
The two firms above are intermediaries operating at uniform rates from a published price
list. Where an intermediary sets rates by individual screening, a third term is added
to~\eqref{eq:fee-decomp}.

Merchant fees for payment processing vary with the form of transaction, volume, the goods
sold, and the incidence of fraud and chargebacks; for domestic card payments 3--6\% is the
usual guide. That variation is not rent from market power but the price of the
\emph{transfer} and \emph{pooling} of Section~\ref{sec:three-ops}. From the merchant's side
the index $i$ is moved and risk handed over; the intermediary bundles many merchants along
$\omega$.

Properly, then,
\begin{equation}
\phi_{\text{fee}} = \phi_{\rm prod} + \underbrace{\phi_{\rm risk}}_{\text{price of transfer and pooling}} + \phi_{\rm barg}
\label{eq:fee-decomp3}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_{\rm risk}$ is not a new component but, as Remark~\ref{rem:phi-risk} states, the
undivided sum of $\phi_{\rm prod}$ and $\phi_{\rm barg}$. For the two firms in this section
$\phi_{\rm risk}\approx 0$, which is why it reduced to two terms.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The risk component: untested}
\label{sec:pred-J}

For the second term of~\eqref{eq:fee-decomp3}, an implication follows from
Proposition~\ref{prop:pooling}.

As Remark~\ref{rem:independence-finite} states, the limit of~\eqref{eq:pooling} bottoms out at
$\rho\sigma^2$. An intermediary can pool away risks that are independent within an industry
but cannot remove the part correlated through a common factor.

Proposition~\ref{prop:pooling} assumes $\rho$ does not depend on $n$
(Remark~\ref{rem:rho-endogenous}). If an intermediary takes on industries with higher
correlation as it adds merchants, $\rho$ rises and the effect appears more strongly than
stated here. \textbf{Dropping the assumption does not change the sign.}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Registered implication}: differences in payment-processing rates across industries
correspond not to the mean chargeback rate $\mu$ of the industry but to
\textbf{the within-industry correlation $\rho$}. An industry with a merely high mean can pass
the cost through, whereas an industry structured so that the whole of it deteriorates at once
requires an additional premium.
\end{quote}

This splits the explanation ``the rate is high because the risk is high'' in two. Whether the
high rates for information products or travel are due to an average incident rate or to the
whole industry deteriorating together has different implications for the design of $\Phi$:
the former can be passed through in price, the latter cannot.

Testability is constrained as set out in Remark~\ref{rem:predJ-infeasible}.

\begin{remark}[Why this implication cannot be tested]
\label{rem:predJ-infeasible}
Estimating $\rho$ requires time series of chargeback rates by industry, which are internal
data of intermediaries and the card networks and are not published. Even $\mu$ is not
published.

Furthermore, \textbf{industries with high $\rho$ are screened out at merchant onboarding}.
The rates observed are a distribution after industries with high $\rho$ have been excluded,
so the conditioning on survival of Section~\ref{sec:survivorship} operates here too. The
obstacle is double, and in the taxonomy of Section~\ref{sec:obstacle-taxonomy} it is a
combination of (iii), not identified, and (iv), access constraint.

The proposition that correlation fixes the limit of pooling is itself not new. Solvency
regulation for insurers and capital regulation for banks compute capital requirements with
explicit correlations between risk categories, implementing the same structure
as~\eqref{eq:pooling}. This text's contribution is only the perspective of applying it to the
setting of rates for a $\Phi$.
\end{remark}
In the three-way decomposition of Chapter~\ref{sec:surplus}, a fee contains both
$\phi_{\rm prod}$ (the functions actually performed) and $\phi_{\rm barg}$ (transfer), and
Gumroad's design gave the means of separating them.

This is consistent with the durability claim of Section~\ref{sec:layers}:
\textbf{the part deriving from market power is eroded by the institutional layer}.
The App Store's cut from 30\% to 15\% was a product of regulatory pressure, whereas the part
deriving from function (Gumroad's 20 points for acquisition) has not been eroded.

\section{Limits of the measurement}

The rates cover only major platforms in Japan and the United States, and no population was
declared. By the discipline of Section~\ref{sec:frame} this is a comparison of cases, not an
estimate. The within-platform comparison on Gumroad is an identification that does not depend
on the population, however, and that part alone is robust.

Effective rates also depend on transaction size. Because of the fixed components (50¢, 30¢),
the effective rate of merchant-of-record models rises at low transaction values. The table is
computed at \$50.

\section{How the institutional layer acts}
\label{sec:institution-mode}

Section~\ref{sec:layers} stated that the $\phi_{\rm barg}$ deriving from market power is
eroded by the institutional layer. This section checks how it acts, in four cases.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}lXl@{}}
\toprule
Domain & Action of the institutional layer & Current state \\
\midrule
Property brokerage & statutory cap on commission (1970 notification, 3\% + 60{,}000 yen) & in force; the cap was raised in 2024 \\
Fee-charging placement & capped fee (about 11\% of wages) & in force but used in 0.18\% of cases \\
Securities commissions & fixed commissions under exchange rules & \textbf{abolished entirely in 1999} \\
App distribution & pressure through antitrust litigation & the rate cut from 30\% to 15\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\captionof{table}{Four forms in which the institutional layer acts on fee rates, and their current state.}
\label{tab:fee-institution-modes}
\end{center}

\textbf{Capping a rate is rare, and the trend is towards abolition.} Securities commissions
were liberalized above 1bn yen in 1994, above 50m in 1998, and entirely in October 1999. The
United States did the same in 1975 and the United Kingdom in 1986.

In property brokerage the cap can be seen to bind. The July 2024 amendment raised the cap for
properties up to 8m yen from 180{,}000 to 300{,}000 yen, and the stated background was that
247 municipalities --- 14\% of the total --- had no licensed brokerage at all, and that the
business was not viable at the existing commission.
\textbf{Supply disappeared because the cap was too low}, which is direct evidence that the
cap binds.

\begin{remark}[From imposing caps to manipulating competitive conditions]
\label{rem:institution-shift}
Of the four cases, only the first two regulate the rate directly. In securities commissions,
the \emph{abolition} of fixed commissions cut rates to between one seventh and one tenth of
their pre-liberalization level. In app distribution, litigation pressure prompted a voluntary
reduction.

All four cases here concern rates, so of the three forms of
Remark~\ref{rem:institution-modes} they fall under price regulation and manipulation of
competitive conditions; entry requirements do not appear.
\textbf{The latter dominates in recent years.} Forms that regulate rates directly are in
decline and are being replaced by manipulation of competitive conditions.
\end{remark}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Registered implication}: the fewer the dependencies in a configuration, the smaller

events must be built oneself, however, and that is where the work concentrates.

\subsection{Questions abandoned}

Direct measurement of the unmanned operating period lacks a population frame for this
chapter's objects. No list of solo-developed services exists, and collecting those that
stopped after the fact reproduces the conditioning on survival of
Section~\ref{sec:survivorship} exactly.

\textbf{The abandonment is specific to the objects, however.} In the ``inside'' partition of
Chapter~\ref{ch:protocol}, the protocol record is itself the population, and duration and
cessation rates can be measured on the full population
(Section~\ref{sec:protocol-byproducts}). What cannot be measured is solo-developed services,
not the unmanned operating period as such.

Transitions between families are similar. Cases that transitioned can be collected, but cases
that did not and cases that failed cannot be observed, so nothing can be said about the
effectiveness of the route.

The rate of unmannedness itself is an ill-posed proposition. Defining what fraction of
$\delta$ is automated requires a unit for $\delta$, and none exists. The defect has the same
form as that of the measurability hypothesis in Section~\ref{sec:predB-illposed}.

\section{Limits of this chapter}

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item $\lambda_{\rm novel}$ was not measured. Estimating the unmanned operating period
depends on that value, and there is no basis for it.

\item Chapter~\ref{sec:platform-fee} is a comparison of cases with no declared population.
Apart from the within-platform identification, it must not be treated as an estimate.

\item The implication registered in this chapter states the same relation as one registered
in Chapter~\ref{ch:protocol}. This was not noticed at the time of registration. The test of
$\Phi$1 \textbf{rejected} it (Section~\ref{sec:protocol-results}). It was not tested on this
chapter's objects.

\item $\lambda_{\rm novel}$ was not decomposed into its four components. What
Section~\ref{sec:protocol-byproducts} measured is the rate of response, not a breakdown by
component.
\end{enumerate}
